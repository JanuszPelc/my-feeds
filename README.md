<h2><img src="images/tech-policy.png" width="28" align="texttop" /> &nbsp;Jakub T. Jankiewicz Argues All AI-Generated Code Should Be Open Source</h2>
<a name="story-27573"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fres.cloudinary.com%2Fdyd911kmh%2Fimage%2Fupload%2Fv1676548360%2FOpen_AI_Products_618960c677.png&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Software developer Jakub T. Jankiewicz argues that all code generated by artificial intelligence should be released as open source. He contends that because AI models are trained on scraped open-source repositories and crowd-sourced platforms like Stack Overflow without proper attribution, withholding the resulting output as proprietary software amounts to license laundering.</p><p>Jankiewicz also points to legal precedents, noting that purely AI-generated works lack human authorship and therefore generally cannot be copyrighted, as established by digital copyright disputes and policies such as Wikimedia Commons' public domain tagging for AI media. He suggests that releasing AI-assisted code under open-source licenses or Creative Commons Zero addresses international legal discrepancies and honors the collaborative foundations of modern software development.</p>

<h2><img src="images/ai-news.png" width="28" align="texttop" /> &nbsp;Chinese AI Models Show Fingerprints of NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs</h2>
<a name="story-27352"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Funemploymentlabs.com%2Fcheckpoint-forensics%2Fimg%2Ffig1.png&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Forensic analysis of publicly released AI models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group reveals numerical fingerprints matching NVIDIA’s export-controlled Blackwell GPUs. Six models released since April 2026 exhibit patterns specific to Blackwell-class hardware, such as the B200, B300, GB200, and GB300, whereas earlier models from these developers match the older Hopper generation.</p><p>The research identifies these patterns by analyzing the arrangement of initial weights generated via the Philox pseudorandom number generator in the PyTorch framework. This method detects the specific thread counts and streaming multiprocessor (SM) layouts used during model initialization. While providing technical evidence of hardware use, the analysis does not establish the physical location of the chips or how they were obtained.</p>

<h2><img src="images/tech-consumer.png" width="28" align="texttop" /> &nbsp;Steinar H. Gunderson Solves the Ancient Norse Fox Board Game</h2>
<p>Software engineer Steinar H. Gunderson has completely solved the ancient Norse asymmetric board game known as the Fox Game or halatafl, determining that sheep win with perfect play under standard rules. Using a compact bitboard representation, combinatorial number systems, and parallelized state propagation across 64 cores, Gunderson analyzed over two trillion possible board states over a 36-hour run.</p><p>The implementation required approximately 1,000 lines of code with no external dependencies, leveraging symmetry optimizations to manage memory and reduce the search space. Gunderson found that sheep maintain a forced win even when reduced to 19 or 18 pieces, while 11 sheep suffice against a single fox, conclusively settling a question left open by previous AI analyses.</p>
