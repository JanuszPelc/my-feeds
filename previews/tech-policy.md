<a name="story-25569"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fasset.kompas.com%2Fcrops%2F4Gr-qfFMC-SlOdf18redZJx3hzg%3D%2F99x0%3A918x546%2F1200x800%2Fdata%2Fphoto%2F2026%2F01%2F06%2F695c450c19f23.webp&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Defense tech startup Anduril Industries and chipmaker Qualcomm have unveiled BlueChip, a new line of laptops designed and assembled in the United States for government and military use. <h2>Stanford Professors File Copyright Lawsuit Against Anthropic Over Claude Training</h2>
<a name="story-25613"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fchatgptiseatingtheworld.com%2Fwp-content%2Fuploads%2F2026%2F05%2FScreen-Shot-2026-05-14-at-2.34.58-AM.png&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Stanford professors Tobias Wolff and Chang-rae Lee are among a group of authors who opted out of the $1.5 billion Bartz v. Anthropic copyright class-action settlement to file a separate lawsuit, Cruz v. Anthropic. The new complaint alleges that Anthropic illegally scraped and pirated copyrighted works from shadow libraries to train its Claude artificial intelligence models.</p><p>While the Bartz ruling previously found that using books to train large language models constituted fair use, it blocked the creation of a permanent central library using pirated files. The plaintiffs in Cruz v. Anthropic are seeking statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed work, significantly higher than the payout provided under the class-action settlement.</p>

<h2>Silicon Valley AI Political Factions Divide Over 2026 Midterm Spending Strategy</h2>
<a name="story-25545"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.wired.com%2Fphotos%2F692f741352fbb4b6e88b7f25%2Fmaster%2Fw_2560%2Cc_limit%2FPol_Midterms_GettyImages-1483069362.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Political action committees and super PACs backed by Silicon Valley tech heavyweights have divided into competing factions over regulatory strategy in the 2026 congressional midterms. Groups advocating for rapid AI innovation and uniform federal rules have spent approximately $8 million in general-election races, outspending rival factions pushing for strict safety guardrails and state-level authority, which have deployed roughly $6.9 million according to Federal Election Commission data.</p><p>Modeled after cryptocurrency industry spending, initial efforts sought to build a unified political machine, but intense infighting and high-profile clashes—such as a $23 million primary battle in New York—have triggered voter backlash. Consequently, overall spending slowed sharply heading into November, with major backers scaling back their contributions amid mounting reputational risks and public skepticism toward unhindered AI expansion.</p>

<h2>Missileer Watch Tracks Military Emergency Action Messages on Shortwave Radio</h2>
<a name="story-25380"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fi.ytimg.com%2Fvi%2FMHPUYNloJTA%2Fmaxresdefault.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Missileer Watch is an online platform that provides a live shortwave radio feed and historical archive of United States military Emergency Action Messages broadcast over the High Frequency Global Communications System on 11175 kHz. The site makes intercepted tactical broadcasts accessible to the public alongside crowd-corrected transcripts and aircraft tracking data.</p><p>Developed by former 321st Missile Squadron combat crew member Daniel Frenkel, the project offers transparency into military communication channels without compromising operational security, as the underlying tactical messages remain heavily encrypted. Operating with minimal tracking and open-source licensing, the platform serves as a resource for radio enthusiasts and researchers monitoring strategic command traffic.</p>

<h2>Steve Blank: Starlink Restrictions Spark European Push to Replace US Technology</h2>
<a name="story-25308"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.cybernews.com%2Fimages%2F750w%2F2026%2F03%2FSatellite-Open-Cosmos.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>In an essay examining the geopolitical fallout of recent U.S. actions in Ukraine, entrepreneur and educator Steve Blank argues that the throttling of Starshield has shattered European complacency regarding reliance on American technology. He contends that a unilateral decision by an American executive to degrade a partner's communications has exposed critical national security vulnerabilities, proving to European policymakers that outsourcing digital infrastructure carries unacceptably high sovereign risks.</p><p>Blank notes that while digital sovereignty was once seen as an insurmountable challenge, advances in artificial intelligence now lower the cost of replicating U.S. software and enterprise systems. He predicts that this shift will permanently damage American technology companies as European nations actively build domestic alternatives, ultimately costing U.S. firms billions in lost revenue and diminishing American technological leadership.</p>
