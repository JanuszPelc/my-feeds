<p>Emeritus Professor Chris Dickman, a leading figure in Australian ecology and wildlife conservation, died on November 17, 2026. Known for his extensive research in the Simpson Desert and his work with the "ratcatchers" team, Dickman specialized in small marsupial populations and their roles in structuring ecological communities. He is widely recognized for co-leading the research that estimated three billion animals were impacted by Australia's 2019-20 bushfire crisis and for his early advocacy regarding dingo management and feral predator control.</p><p>Dickman’s career was marked by high-level leadership, including serving as president of the Australian Mammal Society and receiving the ESA Gold Medal. He was a highly regarded mentor who helped shape the careers of numerous ecologists and conservationists through his teaching and field leadership. In honor of his legacy, a celebratory forum and a festschrift—a collection of tributes from colleagues—were organized to commemorate his contributions to wildlife conservation and scientific communication.</p>

<h2>Evolutionary Origins of the First Animal on Earth</h2>
<a name="story-28317"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fcdn0.animalwised.com%2Fen%2Fposts%2F7%2F9%2F3%2Fthe_origin_and_evolution_of_animals_4397_600.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Biologists have not identified the specific first animal to live on Earth, but they define animals as multi-celled organisms with specialized cells that communicate and share functions like movement, eating, and reproduction. Current research focuses on the deepest branches of the evolutionary tree, specifically debating whether sponges or comb jellies were the first to diverge from a common ancestor approximately 650 million years ago.</p><p>While fossils of soft-bodied early organisms are rarely preserved, genetic analysis of DNA architecture reveals how animals may have been "assembled" from existing single-celled "tool kits" found in relatives like choanoflagellates. By comparing these single-celled organisms to sponge choanocytes, scientists can better understand the transition from independent cells to the first coordinated multicellular life.</p>

<h2>Synthetic Allomelanin from Chernobyl Fungus Could Neutralize Nerve Agents</h2>
<a name="story-28318"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fimages.theconversation.com%2Ffiles%2F771250%2Foriginal%2Ffile-20261111-64-6umzrg.jpg%3Fixlib%3Drb-4.1.1%26q%3D45%26auto%3Dformat%26w%3D754%26fit%3Dclip&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers have developed a material based on allomelanin, a nitrogen-free pigment produced by the radiation-resistant fungus Cladosporium sphaerospermum. By synthesizing allomelanin into nanoparticles and loading them with zirconium clusters, the team created a substance capable of trapping and chemically breaking down organophosphorus compounds, including deadly nerve agents like sarin and various agricultural pesticides.</p><p>Unlike current protective gear that merely traps toxins, this material employs "molecular scissors" to neutralize them, with reaction speeds significantly enhanced by sunlight and built-in alkaline groups. The study suggests these nanoparticles could be integrated into textiles to create proactive protective uniforms, gloves, and masks for soldiers and farmworkers facing chemical hazards.</p>
