<h2>Rubin Observatory Discovers Two Milky Way Satellite Galaxies</h2>
<a name="story-26744"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Frubinobservatory.org%2F_next%2Fimage%3Furl%3Dhttps%3A%2F%2FRubin.canto.com%2Fdirect%2Fimage%2Fhrarstciph4ffdsts3jveq176c%2FQ4joXwX1ztcsjLCEF824lD7ctvo%2Fm800%2F2050%26w%3D3840%26q%3D85&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Astronomers using data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory have discovered two previously unknown companions of the Milky Way: an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy named Aquarius IV and a star cluster named Rubin-GC1. Identified from preview observations covering just 7% of the night sky, these objects highlight the telescope's capacity to detect extremely faint stellar structures.</p><p>The findings suggest that hundreds of undiscovered satellite galaxies may surround the Milky Way, waiting to be revealed during the observatory's 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time. Uncovering these hidden companions could help researchers test models of galaxy formation, trace the Milky Way's assembly history, and investigate the distribution and nature of dark matter.</p>

<h2>University of Würzburg Chemists Isolate Stable Oxoboranes After 60 Years</h2>
<a name="story-26746"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fscx2.b-cdn.net%2Fgfx%2Fnews%2Fhires%2F2026%2Fchemists-discover-and.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at the University of Würzburg have successfully synthesized and isolated oxoboranes, a class of reactive molecules featuring a triple bond between boron and oxygen that had eluded chemists for over 60 years. Led by Professor Holger Braunschweig, the team serendipitously produced the stable compounds while investigating reactions between diboracumulenes and ketones in the laboratory.</p><p>Long considered too unstable to isolate under ordinary conditions, oxoboranes were generated as unexpected orange and yellow crystalline solids using relatively simple starting materials. The approach successfully yielded three distinct oxoboranes with computational support from Tezpur University researchers, pointing toward potential new pathways for synthesizing other elusive chemical compounds across the periodic table.</p>

<h2>Astronomers Discover Double Debris Rings Around White Dwarf J1328−1450</h2>
<p>Astronomers using the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope have discovered two distinct rings of planetary debris surrounding the white dwarf J1328−1450. Unlike typical single-disc systems around dead stars, this structure features two stable, concentric rings separated by a gap, indicating a complex arrangement of rocky remnants.</p><p>Researchers suspect the double-ring configuration is maintained by two surviving planetary bodies acting as gravitational shepherds, one asteroid-sized and the other potentially as large as Pluto or the Moon. Chemical analysis shows the rings contain rocky planetary materials with distinct compositions, providing rare insight into the violent destruction of planetary systems and a potential preview of our Solar System's distant future.</p>

<h2>Physical Superintelligence Proves Aaronson-Ambainis Conjecture</h2>

<p>Physical Superintelligence (PSI) has formally proven the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, settling a fundamental open problem in quantum complexity theory regarding whether quantum computers can achieve exponential speedups on random inputs. Stanford researchers who recently proved a special case of the prediction are currently reviewing the full proof, which builds upon prior work in boolean function analysis and probabilistic combinatorics.</p>

<h2>Magnetic Fields May Allow White Dwarfs to Exceed the Chandrasekhar Limit</h2>
<p>New simulations from the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) suggest that white dwarfs can exceed the 1.4 solar mass Chandrasekhar limit if they possess strong internal magnetic fields. As these stellar remnants accumulate matter from companion stars, increasing density strengthens their internal magnetic fields, providing the pressure necessary to counteract gravity and support masses as high as 2.4 solar masses.</p><p>This phenomenon may explain the unexpected brightness of certain Type Ia supernovae, which serve as "standardizable candles" for measuring cosmic expansion. By altering the traditional relationship between a white dwarf's mass and size, magnetic pressure offers a potential explanation for observed anomalies in stellar radii and the luminosity of exploding stars.</p>

<h2>KAIST and Georgia Tech Researchers Develop Membrane for Room-Temperature Crude Oil Separation</h2>
<a name="story-26539"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fpub.mdpi-res.com%2Fpolymers%2Fpolymers-14-00980%2Farticle_deploy%2Fhtml%2Fimages%2Fpolymers-14-00980-g003.png%3F1646216256&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology and the Georgia Institute of Technology have developed a room-temperature crude oil separation method using an inexpensive, uncoated porous polyacrylonitrile membrane. As crude oil flows through the filter, heavier hydrocarbon molecules naturally accumulate along the pores to form sub-2-nanometer channels, effectively turning membrane fouling into a self-assembly mechanism that separates lighter fractions like gasoline and kerosene.</p><p>Traditional crude oil refining relies on thermal distillation, which consumes roughly 1,100 terawatt-hours annually worldwide. Process simulations indicate that integrating the modular membrane system could reduce refinery energy consumption by 31.6 percent and greenhouse gas emissions by 37.6 percent without requiring the complete replacement of existing distillation infrastructure. Beyond petroleum refining, the technology could potentially apply to plastic recycling pyrolysis oil purification, pharmaceutical processing, and biofuel production.</p>
