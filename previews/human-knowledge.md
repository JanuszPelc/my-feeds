<h2>University of Warsaw and International Team Demonstrate Room-Temperature Quantum Supersolid</h2>
<p>An international research team has demonstrated a quantum supersolid—a state of matter combining the orderly crystal structure of a solid with the frictionless flow of a superfluid—at room temperature. Rather than using ultracold atomic systems, the scientists generated hybrid quasiparticles called exciton polaritons by coupling light and matter inside an organic semiconductor and liquid-crystal microcavity.</p><p>Published in Science Advances, the findings involve collaborators from the University of Warsaw, the Military University of Technology, IBM Research Zurich, and CNRS. The team identified the supersolid phase by observing spontaneous symmetry breaking and regularly spaced density variations in the emitted light. The platform's ability to operate at room temperature and offer electrical tunability provides a versatile tool for exploring exotic quantum phenomena and advancing future optical computing technologies.</p>
<p>Nov 9 · <a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105224536.htm">sciencedaily.com</a></p>

<h2>Cary Institute Study Links Acorns and Mouse Populations to Lyme Disease Risk</h2>
<a name="story-26902"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.globallymealliance.org%2Fhubfs%2FImported_Blog_Media%2Fnews_acorns_lyme_ostfeld.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>A 35-year ecological study conducted by researchers at the Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, reveals that white-footed mouse populations and oak tree acorn production are significantly stronger predictors of nymphal tick abundance than white-tailed deer populations. The long-term data show that heavy acorn crops provide abundant food for forest rodents, enabling mouse populations to surge and subsequently drive up nymphal tick numbers two years later.</p><p>Despite the clear link between mice and overall tick abundance, high mouse populations do not consistently predict a higher proportion of infected ticks, as other lesser-transmitting host species dilute the pathogen's prevalence within the ecosystem. Furthermore, the findings indicate that blacklegged ticks survive temperature extremes more effectively than previously assumed by sheltering in soil and leaf litter, complicating traditional assumptions about how weather and deer impact Lyme disease exposure.</p>
<p>Nov 9 · <a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261106174518.htm">sciencedaily.com</a></p>

<h2>Scripps Institution of Oceanography Identifies Viscosity of Marine Snow as Carbon Transport Factor</h2>
<a name="story-26860"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.sciencedaily.com%2Fimages%2F1920%2Fviscous-marine-snow-particles.webp&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at UC San Diego's Scripps Institution of Oceanography and the University of Lincoln have found that the viscosity of marine snow—the sinking clumps of organic debris in the ocean—significantly affects how much carbon reaches the deep seafloor. Using 3D confocal microscopy and fluorescent molecules, the team observed that stickier, more viscous particles resist bacterial degradation longer, allowing them to transport up to six times more carbon into the deep ocean than thinner particles.</p><p>The findings, published in Science with a companion study in Nature Communications, reveal a complex microscopic landscape in ocean mucus that governs how bacteria interact with organic matter. This improved understanding of marine snow dynamics helps clarify a long-standing puzzle in the global carbon cycle and offers insights into other biological systems where bacteria encounter mucus, such as coral reefs and human digestive tracts.</p>

<h2>Laimosemion laranja: New Orange-Marked Amazon Killifish Species Discovered</h2>
<p>Researchers in Brazil have discovered a new species of killifish, named Laimosemion laranja, in the Rio Cajari drainage of northern Amapá state. Measuring under three centimeters, the tiny freshwater fish features distinctive bright orange markings on its body and was confirmed as a new species through morphological and mitochondrial DNA analyses.</p><p>Found in only three small forest pools with a total known range of about 25 square kilometers, the species faces severe pressure from deforestation, mining, and urban development. Because of its highly restricted habitat and multiple local threats, researchers recommend classifying it as endangered and expanding nearby reserve boundaries to protect its environment.</p>

<p>Researchers from McGill University and the Centro de Investigaciones Paleontológicas have proposed a new evolutionary model called a "Biotic Inflection" following an analysis of fossils from Colombia's Paja Formation. The researchers attribute this rare coexistence to favorable environmental conditions such as warm greenhouse temperatures, high sea levels, nutrient-rich coastal waters, and newly opened ocean connections between the eastern Pacific and early Atlantic.</p>

<h2>Regensburg Center and Max Planck Institute Researchers Demonstrate Space-Time Limit for Electrons</h2>
<a name="story-26812"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwallpaperaccess.com%2Ffull%2F47178.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at the Regensburg Center for Ultrafast Nanoscopy and the Max Planck Institute have experimentally demonstrated a fundamental physical limit governing electron behavior, known as the space-time limit. Using ultrafast lasers and scanning tunneling microscopy, the team observed that an electron's location and its behavior over time cannot both be determined with arbitrary precision, as improving temporal resolution causes the electron's quantum wave packet to spread out spatially.</p><p>The experiments revealed that electrons moving across a microscopic gap experience a 500-attosecond delay in responding to light fields while behaving as quantum waves capable of tunneling through energy barriers. While operating ultrafast electronics at the intrinsic speed limit of electron motion remains a long-term technological goal, the findings could eventually help researchers control chemical reactions and develop advanced quantum information systems.</p>
