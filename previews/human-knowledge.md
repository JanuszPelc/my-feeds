<h2>Oak Ridge National Laboratory Researchers Find Mechanical Strain Alters Altermagnet Magnetism</h2>
<a name="story-26412"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fpub.mdpi-res.com%2Fmagnetism%2Fmagnetism-05-00017%2Farticle_deploy%2Fhtml%2Fimages%2Fmagnetism-05-00017-g001.png%3F1753256683&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at the Department of Energy's Oak Ridge National Laboratory have discovered that applying a small amount of mechanical pressure to altermagnets can dramatically alter their magnetic and electrical properties. By compressing tiny crystals of manganese telluride and iron sulfide between titanium plates and analyzing them with neutron diffraction, the team found that strain shifts the populations of magnetic domains and influences the anomalous Hall effect.</p><p>This method provides a new way to control magnetic behavior without modifying a material's chemical composition. The capability could support the development of advanced spintronic technologies for applications in quantum computing and wireless communications, with findings detailed across recent publications in Physical Review X and Advanced Materials.</p>

<h2>MIT Researchers Use Flexible Connectors to Create Novel Nanoparticle Superlattices</h2>
<a name="story-26411"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Ffigures.academia-assets.com%2F110919285%2Ffigure_002.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Using flexible, electrically charged molecular connectors instead of traditional rigid ligands allows for the creation of novel nanoparticle crystal structures. By permitting these connectors to bend and adjust during assembly, researchers can control the number of connections—or valency—each particle makes, facilitating the formation of more open and complex superlattices.</p><p>This discovery addresses a long-standing challenge in materials science where rigid connections often limited assembly to densely packed, uniform arrangements. This method enables the engineering of designer materials with tailored properties for use in electronics, medicine, energy storage, and chemical catalysis.</p>

<h2>SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory Researchers Confirm Proof of Altermagnetism</h2>
<a name="story-26410"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.licdn.com%2Fdms%2Fimage%2Fv2%2FD4E22AQGT_Q_qckd0Zw%2Ffeedshare-shrink_800%2FB4EZnTdlC4GUAk-%2F0%2F1760189371321%3Fe%3D2147483647%26v%3Dbeta%26t%3DA1PVWrh3uBKee4Tq5DXepWejm-YEt8aZ-fLVIE9l0Yk&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers at the U.S. Department of Energy's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory have provided conclusive experimental evidence confirming altermagnetism, a third major phase of magnetism. Using circularly polarized X-rays at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, the team detected a distinct chiral signature in manganese telluride, a material previously classified solely as a conventional antiferromagnet.</p><p>While traditional ferromagnets produce external magnetic fields and antiferromagnets feature opposing spins that completely cancel out magnetization, altermagnets combine zero net magnetization with the ability to generate spin-polarized currents. This unique configuration offers stability against external magnetic interference alongside efficient spin-based information transport, presenting potential applications for advanced computer memory, quantum devices, and energy-efficient data centers.</p>

<p>Researchers from the Smithsonian's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute and Pennsylvania State University have identified the deadly chytrid fungus as the cause of historic amphibian declines in Brazil's Atlantic Forest. <h2>All Five DNA and RNA Nucleobases Discovered in Asteroid Ryugu Samples</h2>

<p>Researchers analyzing pristine samples returned from the asteroid Ryugu by Japan's Hayabusa2 mission have detected all five fundamental nucleobases—adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, and uracil.
