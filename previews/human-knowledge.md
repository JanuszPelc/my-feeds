<h2>All Five DNA and RNA Nucleobases Discovered in Asteroid Ryugu Samples</h2>
<a name="story-26283"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fscx1.b-cdn.net%2Fcsz%2Fnews%2F800a%2F2026%2Fingredients-of-life-di-1.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers analyzing pristine samples returned from the asteroid Ryugu by Japan's Hayabusa2 mission have detected all five fundamental nucleobases—adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, and uracil. These nitrogen-containing organic molecules serve as the genetic letters of DNA and RNA, combining with sugars and phosphates to form nucleotides.</p><p>The discovery confirms that the building blocks of genetic material were present in primitive carbonaceous asteroids during the early Solar System. While previous meteorite finds on Earth faced potential contamination risks, uncompromised samples from Ryugu and NASA's asteroid Bennu reinforce the hypothesis that space-borne chemistry helped deliver the prebiotic ingredients necessary for life on Earth.</p>

<h2>LHC Collaborations Detect Quark-Gluon Plasma in Oxygen and Neon Collisions</h2>
<a name="story-26252"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fphysics.aps.org%2Fassets%2F1ef376d9-d997-444f-b8dc-b6f74b6fe376%2Fe58_1.png&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>All four major Large Hadron Collider (LHC) research collaborations—ALICE, ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb—have reported evidence of quark-gluon plasma (QGP) emerging from collisions of oxygen and neon nuclei. This finding challenges the long-standing assumption that creating such primordial matter, which existed during the universe's first moments, requires the extreme conditions produced by much heavier nuclei like lead.</p><p>Scientists identified the plasma by observing parton energy loss in particle jets and anisotropic flow in the direction particles travel after impact. These results suggest that QGP can form in relatively small nuclear systems, offering new ways to investigate the intense heat and pressure of the early universe.</p>

<h2>Geolocating Video via Aircraft Doppler Shifts and ADS-B Data</h2>

<h2>IceCube Neutrino Observatory and Particle Physics Examined by UC San Diego Researchers</h2>

<p>UC San Diego physics researchers are examining the properties of neutrinos and the significance of the IceCube Neutrino Observatory following Francis Halzen's 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics. Researchers indicate that analyzing these rare, ultra-high-energy neutrinos—alongside data from instruments like the Simons Observatory—offers vital clues regarding cosmic accelerators, black holes, and the earliest history of the universe.</p>

<h2>Frank Calegari Publishes Essay on Impact of Artificial Intelligence in Mathematics</h2>
<a name="story-26125"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fimg.freepik.com%2Fpremium-photo%2Fcollaborative-problem-solving-with-artificial-intelligence-mathematics_1022111-847.jpg%3Fw%3D2000&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Mathematician Frank Calegari published a guest post on Terence Tao's blog arguing that recent advancements in artificial intelligence—such as OpenAI's release of numerous research papers—fundamentally change the practice of mathematics. Calegari contends that while many mathematicians feel threatened or overwhelmed by AI's sudden capability to tackle major open problems, the mathematical community should instead adapt its reward structures, hiring practices, and educational focus toward broader exploration and conceptual understanding rather than mere proof-generation.</p><p>The essay draws parallels to historical events like the classification of finite simple groups, suggesting that artificial intelligence shifts the economics of what is considered difficult in research. To cope with these changes, Calegari proposes concrete reforms, including evaluating all academic work as potentially AI-assisted, placing less emphasis on publication counts, and fostering deeper personal interactions in academic hiring and graduate training.</p>
