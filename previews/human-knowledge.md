<h2>Study Reveals Nuanced Social Behavior and Associations Among Solitary Black Rhinos</h2>
<a name="story-27487"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fimg.piri.net%2Fresim%2Fupload%2F2018%2F06%2F27%2F12%2F22%2F71d5abbc9.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>An analysis of nearly 70,000 black rhino sightings across 14 reserves reveals that while the species is predominantly solitary and prone to aggression, individuals regularly form peaceful groups with familiar associates. The study found that about 8% of sightings involved groups of three to seven animals, and these interactions typically occurred between rhinos that had previously associated with one another.</p><p>These findings provide critical insights for conservation translocations, where moving animals to new areas can trigger lethal fighting. Researchers suggest that selecting rhinos with existing social bonds and carefully managing the ratio of adult males—which are more frequently avoided by other adults and associated with lower survival rates—can significantly reduce post-release aggression and improve population recovery efforts.</p>

<h2>Study of 125,000-Year-Old Saudi Lakeshore Reveals Early Human Plant Poison Use</h2>
<a name="story-27486"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.staff.universiteitleiden.nl%2Fbinaries%2Fcontent%2Fgallery%2Ful2%2Fmain-images%2Fgeneral%2Fafbeeldingen-in-tekst%2Funsorted%2Fneumark-nord2-excavation.jpg&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Excavations at the ‘Uruq Aljelibah site in southern Saudi Arabia’s Mundafan basin indicate that Homo sapiens occupied a lush freshwater lakeshore environment around 125,000 years ago. They also offer detailed insights into how early human populations adapted and utilized resources during humid climatic phases of Green Arabia before severe arid conditions returned.</p>

<h2>Heinz von Foerster’s 1960 Doomsday Prediction Offers Lessons for Modern Scientific Forecasting</h2>
<a name="story-27165"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fmedia.cheggcdn.com%2Fmedia%2F402%2F402e9f20-a20f-4ed7-b2ec-96ef1eb88548%2FphpPmL0TI&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>In 1960, physicist Heinz von Foerster and colleagues published a paper in Science predicting that global population growth would lead to "doomsday" on November 13, 2026. The prediction was intentionally engineered using a mathematical equation fitted to historical data to demonstrate that exponential growth rates were unsustainable, which subsequently fueled a wave of overpopulation alarmism and justified harmful policies such as forced sterilizations and China's one-child policy.</p><p>Decades later, as many nations confront declining birth rates and aging populations rather than overpopulation, the 1960 prediction serves as a cautionary tale about modeling limitations and the risks of blending science with advocacy. The analysis underscores that modern catastrophe predictions should be approached with humility, transparency, and rigorous scrutiny to prevent repeating past forecasting failures.</p>
