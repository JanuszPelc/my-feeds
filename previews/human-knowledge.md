<h2>Plant Scientist Gregory Moore Shares Tips on Keeping Cut Flowers Fresh</h2>
<a name="story-28256"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fimages.theconversation.com%2Ffiles%2F772350%2Foriginal%2Ffile-20261117-64-g4gnf4.jpg%3Fixlib%3Drb-4.1.1%26q%3D45%26auto%3Dformat%26w%3D754%26fit%3Dclip&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Plant scientist Gregory Moore explains that understanding biological clocks and water transport in stems can help significantly prolong the vase life of cut flowers. When stems are severed, the internal water flow stops, and air blockages or lack of hydration can cause rapid wilting if proper precautions are ignored.</p><p>To maximize freshness, Moore recommends selecting plump buds or fully open blooms, cutting two to three centimeters off stems at a 45-degree angle, and refreshing the vase water regularly. Combining sugar, an acid such as vinegar or lemon juice, and a small amount of bleach can supply essential carbohydrates while preventing bacterial and fungal growth.</p>

<h2>Plasma-Processing Technology Extracts Oxygen From Lunar Soil</h2>
<a name="story-28110"><img src="https://wsrv.nl/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fimages.theconversation.com%2Ffiles%2F770640%2Foriginal%2Ffile-20261108-64-ke9kwg.jpeg%3Fixlib%3Drb-4.1.1%26q%3D45%26auto%3Dformat%26w%3D754%26fit%3Dclip&amp;w=1200&amp;h=1200&amp;fit=inside&amp;we&amp;output=jpg&amp;bg=white" width="30%" align="right" /></a>
<p>Researchers have demonstrated a method for extracting oxygen from lunar soil simulants using plasma-processing technology, a technique commonly employed in semiconductor manufacturing. By exposing artificial regolith to plasmas generated from industrial gases, the study showed that oxygen and oxygen-containing gases can be released from minerals. This approach offers a lower-temperature alternative to high-energy methods for breaking the chemical bonds within lunar minerals.</p><p>The process leverages decades of industrial experience with fluorine plasmas, providing a "dry" method that avoids the logistical challenges of transporting liquid chemicals to space. While current oxygen yields must be significantly improved to support practical lunar bases, the research suggests the technology could eventually be adapted to extract other valuable lunar resources like silicon, aluminum, and iron.</p>
